## 1. INSTRUMENTS YOU NEED

A **geometry set**: a **ruler**, a **pair of compasses**, a **protractor**, a **set square**
and a **sharp pencil**.

---

## 2. PARALLEL AND PERPENDICULAR LINES

**Parallel lines:** lines that run in the same direction and **never meet**, however far
they are drawn. They stay the same distance apart. The sign is **‖**.

```
--------------------------->
--------------------------->
```

Examples around us: the two long edges of a ruler, railway lines, the opposite sides of a
door, lines on a ruled exercise book.

**Perpendicular lines:** two lines that meet or cross at a **right angle (90°)**. The sign is
**⊥**.

```
        |
        |
--------+--------
        |
        |
```

Examples around us: the corner of a window, the corner of a book, a wall meeting the floor.

---

## 3. ANGLES

**Angle:** the amount of turning between two lines that meet at a point. It is measured in
**degrees (°)** using a **protractor**.

| Type of angle | Size |
|---|---|
| **Acute** | Less than 90° |
| **Right angle** | Exactly 90° |
| **Obtuse** | More than 90° but less than 180° |
| **Straight angle** | Exactly 180° |
| **Reflex** | More than 180° but less than 360° |
| **Complete turn** | 360° |

### Measuring an angle

1. Put the **centre of the protractor** on the point where the two lines meet.
2. Lay the **base line** of the protractor along one arm.
3. Read the scale that starts at **zero** on that arm.

### Facts about angles

```
Angles on a straight line add up to 180°
Angles at a point add up to 360°
The angles of a triangle add up to 180°
```

**Worked example.** Two angles on a straight line are 115° and x. Find x.

```
x = 180° − 115° = 65°
```

**Worked example.** A triangle has angles of 50° and 60°. Find the third angle.

```
Third angle = 180° − (50° + 60°) = 180° − 110° = 70°
```

## 4. CIRCLES

### Parts of a circle

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Centre** | The middle point of the circle |
| **Radius** | A straight line from the centre to the edge |
| **Diameter** | A straight line through the centre, from edge to edge |
| **Circumference** | The distance all the way round the circle |
| **Chord** | A straight line joining two points on the circle |
| **Arc** | A part of the circumference |
| **Sector** | The part enclosed by two radii and an arc |

```
Diameter = 2 × radius          Radius = diameter ÷ 2
```

**Examples**

```
A circle of radius 3.5 cm has diameter 2 × 3.5 = 7 cm
A circle of diameter 10 cm has radius 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm
```

### Constructing a circle

1. Open the pair of compasses to the size of the **radius** against a ruler.
2. Put the sharp point on the paper — this is the **centre**.
3. Turn the pencil right round without changing the opening.

---

## 5. CONSTRUCTING A REGULAR HEXAGON

A **regular hexagon** has **6 equal sides** and **6 equal angles**. Each interior angle is
**120°**.

**Steps**

1. Draw a circle of any radius, for example 4 cm.
2. **Without changing the compasses**, put the point anywhere on the circle and make a mark
   on the circle.
3. Move the point to that mark and make the next mark. Continue right round.
4. You will get exactly **six marks**.
5. Join the six marks with a ruler.

> Why it works: the radius of a circle steps round the circumference **exactly six times**.

---

## 6. LINES OF FOLDING SYMMETRY

A **line of symmetry** is a fold line that makes the two halves of a shape match exactly.

| Shape | Lines of folding symmetry |
|---|---|
| Square | **4** |
| Rectangle | **2** |
| Equilateral triangle | **3** |
| Isosceles triangle | **1** |
| Scalene triangle | **0** |
| Regular hexagon | **6** |
| Circle | Very many (any line through the centre) |

**Try it:** fold a square piece of paper down the middle, across the middle, and along each
diagonal. All four folds match — so a square has 4 lines of symmetry. A rectangle only
matches on the two middle folds, not on the diagonals.

---

## 7. ROTATION AND REVOLUTION

**Rotation:** turning about a fixed point.

| Turn | Degrees |
|---|---|
| Quarter turn | **90°** |
| Half turn | **180°** |
| Three-quarter turn | **270°** |
| Complete turn (one revolution) | **360°** |

Turning may be **clockwise** (the way the hands of a clock move) or **anticlockwise**.

**Worked example.** A learner faces north and makes a quarter turn clockwise. Which way is
she facing?

```
North → quarter turn clockwise → East
```

**Worked example.** The minute hand of a clock moves from 12 to 6. How many degrees has it
turned?

```
12 to 6 is half a turn = 180°
```

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Parallel lines | Lines that never meet |
| Perpendicular lines | Lines that meet at 90° |
| Angle | The amount of turning between two lines that meet |
| Degree | The unit for measuring angles |
| Protractor | The instrument used to measure angles |
| Radius | A line from the centre of a circle to the edge |
| Diameter | A line through the centre from edge to edge |
| Circumference | The distance round a circle |
| Hexagon | A shape with six sides |
| Line of symmetry | A fold line that makes two halves match exactly |
| Revolution | One complete turn of 360° |

---

# IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- Parallel lines never meet; perpendicular lines meet at **90°**.
- Angles on a straight line = **180°**; angles at a point = **360°**.
- The angles of a triangle add up to **180°**.
- **Diameter = 2 × radius.**
- A regular hexagon has **6 equal sides** and each interior angle is **120°**.
- The radius steps round its own circle exactly **six** times.
- Square = 4 lines of symmetry · rectangle = 2 · equilateral triangle = 3.
- A quarter turn = 90°, a half turn = 180°, a complete revolution = 360°.

---

# EXAMINATION POINTS

- An angle of 65° is **acute**; an angle of 130° is **obtuse**.
- Angles on a straight line: 180° − 115° = **65°**.
- A triangle with 50° and 60° has a third angle of **70°**.
- A circle of radius 3.5 cm has a diameter of **7 cm**.
- A regular hexagon has **6** lines of folding symmetry.
- Facing north and turning 90° clockwise leaves you facing **east**.

---

# COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Reading the wrong scale on the protractor (always start from zero on one arm).
- Calling an angle of exactly 90° an acute angle.
- Using the diameter when the radius is needed to set the compasses.
- Saying a rectangle has 4 lines of symmetry — it has only **2**.
- Changing the compass opening while stepping round a hexagon.
- Turning anticlockwise when the question says clockwise.
