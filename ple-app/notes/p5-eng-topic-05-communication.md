# TOPIC 5: COMMUNICATION

*Curriculum reference: NCDC Primary Five Curriculum, Set One, English Topic 5, 74 periods
(`curriculum/P5-SET-ONE-2010.pdf`, pages 24–27). The syllabus divides this topic into three
sub-topics: **5A The Post Office**, **5B The Telephone** and **5C The Internet**.*

**What you already know:** You have written letters and spoken on a telephone. This is the
**longest topic in P.5 English (74 periods)**. It brings together the language of sending and
receiving messages by **post, by telephone and by e-mail**.

**By the end of this topic you should be able to:**

- name the services of the post office and the words used there
- name the parts of a telephone and use the language of a telephone call
- name the words used on the internet and describe how e-mail works
- use *either … or*, *might* and *unless*
- use *enough … to*, *need to* and *needn't*
- use *too … to*, *in order to* and *so as to*
- write a composition or a short e-mail message about communication

## 2. HOW A LETTER TRAVELS

```
Write the letter  →  put it in an envelope  →  write the address
→  buy and stick a stamp  →  drop it in the letter box
→  the post office sorts the mail  →  it is carried to the other town
→  it is put in the receiver's private box or delivered
```

## 3. GRAMMAR: EITHER … OR, MIGHT, UNLESS

| Structure | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **either … or** | One of two things | Sarah will **either** send a letter **or** send a telegram. |
| **might** | Perhaps; it is possible | She **might** receive her parcel this evening. |
| … **unless** … | If … not | Your letter will not be posted **unless** you fix a postage stamp. |
| **Unless** … , … | If … not | **Unless** you pay for your box rental, it will be closed. |

**Note on *unless*:** *unless* already carries the meaning of *not*, so we never add a second
*not* in the same clause.

- ✗ Unless you don't pay, the box will be closed.
- ✓ **Unless you pay, the box will be closed.**

**Note on *might*:** it is followed by the plain verb — *she might **come***, not *she might
comes*.

## 5. THE LANGUAGE OF A TELEPHONE CALL

| Stage | What to say |
|---|---|
| Answering | *Hullo, this is Sarah speaking.* |
| Asking for someone | *May I speak to the head teacher, please?* |
| Saying who is calling | *This is Mr Okello of Hill Primary School.* |
| Asking to wait | *Hold on, please.* |
| Not available | *I am sorry, he is not in. Would you like to leave a message?* |
| Bad line | *I can't hear you clearly. Could you speak up, please?* |
| Ending | *Thank you very much. Goodbye.* |

**Good telephone manners:** greet first · say who you are · speak clearly and briefly ·
never shout · do not call very late at night · say goodbye before you cut the call.

## 6. GRAMMAR: ENOUGH … TO, NEED TO, NEEDN'T

| Structure | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **enough … to** | As much as is required | She did not have **enough** airtime **to** make a call. |
| **need to** | It is necessary | You **need to** fix a landline at home. |
| **needn't** | It is not necessary | You **needn't** own two telephones. |

**Word order:** *enough* comes **after** an adjective but **before** a noun.

- She is **old enough** to use a telephone. (after the adjective)
- She has **enough airtime** to make a call. (before the noun)

**Careful:** the syllabus prints the example *"She did not enough have airtime to make a
call."* The words are in the wrong order. The correct sentence is **"She did not have enough
airtime to make a call."** The syllabus also prints *"needn't to"*; the correct form is
**needn't** with no *to* — *You needn't own two telephones.*

## 8. GRAMMAR: TOO … TO, IN ORDER TO, SO AS TO

| Structure | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **too … to** | More than is wanted, so it cannot be done | The internet café was **too** congested **to** sit in. |
| **in order to** | For the purpose of | I opened the website **in order to** access my e-mail. |
| **so as to** | For the purpose of | He logged onto his account **so as to** read the news. |

**The difference between *too* and *enough***

| Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| The café was **too** noisy **to** work in. | Cannot work there — there is a problem. |
| The café was quiet **enough to** work in. | Can work there — it is all right. |

## 9. USING E-MAIL SAFELY

- Keep your **password** secret; never give it to anyone.
- Always **sign out** when you finish, especially in an internet café.
- Do not open a message from someone you do not know — it may carry a **virus**.
- Never agree to meet a stranger you have met online. **Tell a trusted adult** at once if
  anyone online asks you for your photograph, your home address or to keep a secret from
  your parents. **It is never the child's fault.**

> *No NCDC guidance document on internet safety for children was available; the safety
> points above are ordinary child-protection advice, given so that the topic can be taught
> responsibly.*

# IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- **Either … or** shows a choice between **two** things.
- **Might** shows possibility and is followed by the plain verb.
- **Unless** means *if … not* — never use another *not* with it.
- **Enough** comes **after** an adjective but **before** a noun.
- **Needn't** is followed by the plain verb, with **no** *to*.
- **Too … to** shows that something **cannot** be done.
- **In order to** and **so as to** both give the **reason**.
- Always **sign out** and keep your password secret.

- I opened the website **in order to** access my e-mail.

---

# COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

- Writing "Unless you don't pay …" — *unless* already means *if not*.
- Writing "She might comes" — *might* takes the plain verb.
- Writing "enough airtime" as "airtime enough".
- Writing "needn't to own" — leave out *to*.
- Using **too** where you mean **very**: *The café was too noisy* means you could **not** work.
- Mixing *either … or* with *neither … nor*.
