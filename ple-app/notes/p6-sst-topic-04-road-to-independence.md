## 1. THE COLONIAL PERIOD

### The explorers

**Explorer:** A person who travels to an unknown place in order to find out about it.

| Explorer | When | What he did |
|---|---|---|
| **Krapf and Rebmann** (missionaries and explorers) | 1840s | The first Europeans to see Mt Kilimanjaro and Mt Kenya |
| **Burton and Speke** | 1857–58 | Reached Lake Tanganyika; Speke reached Lake Victoria |
| **John Hanning Speke and James Grant** | 1862 | Reached the **source of the Nile at Jinja** |
| **Sir Samuel Baker and his wife** | 1864 | Reached Lake Albert and Murchison Falls |
| **Dr David Livingstone** | 1850s–1873 | Explored Central and East Africa and fought the slave trade |
| **Henry Morton Stanley** | 1871, 1875 | Found Livingstone at Ujiji; sailed round Lake Victoria and wrote the letter inviting missionaries to Buganda |

### How and why they came

They came **by sea** to the coast and then walked inland with porters, using caravan routes.
They came to find the **source of the Nile**, to **map** the interior, to look for **raw
materials and markets**, to **spread Christianity**, to help **stop the slave trade**, and
for **adventure and fame**. Many were sent by geographical societies or by their
governments.

### Their achievements

They mapped the lakes, rivers and mountains · they told Europe about East Africa · their
reports helped to **end the slave trade** · they opened routes that later became roads and
towns · they invited missionaries, who started schools and hospitals.

### Challenges they faced

Tropical **diseases** such as malaria and sleeping sickness · **hostile people** who
attacked them · **wild animals** · **thick forests, swamps, rivers and mountains** ·
**language barriers** · shortage of food and clean water · **desertion** by porters ·
long distances on foot.

### Colonialists, settlement and administration

After the **Berlin Conference of 1884–85**, East Africa was shared out:

| Country | Colonial master |
|---|---|
| **Uganda** | Britain (a **Protectorate**, 1894) |
| **Kenya** | Britain (a **Colony**, with white settlers in the "White Highlands") |
| **Tanganyika** | **Germany** first, then Britain after the First World War (as a mandated territory) |
| **Zanzibar** | Britain (a Protectorate) |
| **Rwanda and Burundi** | **Germany** first, then **Belgium** after the First World War |

**Methods of administration:** the British used **indirect rule** — governing through
existing chiefs, as with the Buganda Agreement of 1900. The Germans and the Belgians used
mostly **direct rule**, with their own officers and appointed chiefs, and the Belgians in
Rwanda and Burundi ruled through the king while deepening the divisions between groups.

### Impact of colonialists on the people of East Africa

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Roads, railways and towns were built | Africans **lost their independence** and their land |
| Schools and hospitals were started | Forced labour and heavy **taxes** |
| Christianity, Islam and formal education spread | African **culture was weakened** |
| New cash crops — coffee, tea, cotton, sisal | Africans were made to grow crops for the coloniser |
| The slave trade was stopped | Traditional rulers lost their power |
| Money economy, trade and industries began | Borders divided tribes; new **ethnic divisions** were created |
| Peace was enforced between warring groups | Exploitation of raw materials and cheap labour |

## 2. THE STRUGGLE FOR INDEPENDENCE, COUNTRY BY COUNTRY

### Kenya Colony

- Kenya had many **white settlers**, who had taken the fertile **White Highlands**, so the struggle was hard.
- Early movements: the **Young Kikuyu Association** (Harry Thuku), the **Kikuyu Central Association**, and later **KAU** and **KANU**.
- The **Mau Mau uprising (1952–1960)** was an armed struggle for land and freedom; a state of emergency was declared and **Jomo Kenyatta** was imprisoned.
- Kenya became independent on **12 December 1963**, with **Jomo Kenyatta** as leader, and a republic in 1964.

### Tanganyika (Tanzania) Territory

- Africans resisted the Germans in the **Maji Maji rebellion (1905–1907)**.
- **TANU** (Tanganyika African National Union) was formed in 1954 under **Julius Nyerere**.
- The struggle was mainly **peaceful**, using meetings, newspapers and elections.
- Tanganyika became independent on **9 December 1961** and a republic in 1962.
- Zanzibar became independent in 1963 and had a revolution in January 1964; on **26 April 1964** Tanganyika and Zanzibar united to form **Tanzania**.

### Uganda Protectorate

- Political parties were formed: the **Uganda National Congress (1952)**, the **Democratic Party (DP)**, the **Uganda People's Congress (UPC)** and **Kabaka Yekka (KY)**.
- Farmers' cooperatives, trade unions and the press pressed for self-rule.
- Uganda became independent on **9 October 1962**, with **Milton Obote** as Prime Minister and the **Kabaka, Sir Edward Mutesa II**, as the first President in 1963.

### Burundi and Rwanda

- Both were ruled by **Belgium** after the First World War.
- Both became independent on **1 July 1962**.
- Burundi began as a kingdom and became a republic in 1966; Rwanda became a republic at independence.
- Both suffered serious ethnic conflict afterwards, the worst being the **1994 genocide against the Tutsi in Rwanda**.

### Why the Africans wanted independence

To **rule themselves** · to regain their **land** · to stop **forced labour and heavy
taxes** · to end **racial discrimination** · to control their own **resources** · to
restore **African dignity and culture** · to get better services, jobs and pay.

### Methods used in the struggle

Forming **political parties** and **trade unions** · **strikes and boycotts** ·
**petitions and delegations** to the colonial government and to Britain · use of
**newspapers** and public rallies · **armed struggle** where peaceful means failed, as in
the Mau Mau · taking part in **elections** when they were allowed.

## 3. POST-INDEPENDENCE EAST AFRICA

### The first leaders of the independent countries

| Country | First leader at independence |
|---|---|
| Kenya | **Jomo Kenyatta** |
| Tanganyika / Tanzania | **Julius Nyerere** |
| Uganda | **Milton Obote** (Prime Minister); Sir Edward Mutesa II, first President |
| Rwanda | **Grégoire Kayibanda** |
| Burundi | **Mwami Mwambutsa IV** (as king at independence) |

### Formation of republics

**Republic:** A country in which the head of state is a president chosen by the people, not a king.

Tanganyika became a republic in **1962**, Uganda in **1963** (and again under the 1967
constitution), Kenya in **1964**, Burundi in **1966**, and Rwanda at independence in
**1962**.

### Political parties today

Parties change with time, so learn the idea rather than only the names. Tanzania's ruling
party has been **CCM (Chama Cha Mapinduzi)** since 1977; Uganda's is the **NRM**; Rwanda's
is the **RPF**; Burundi's is the **CNDD–FDD**; Kenya has many parties that form coalitions
at each election. All five countries today allow **multiparty politics**.

### Challenges of post-independence East Africa

**Military coups** and unstable governments · **dictatorship** and abuse of human rights ·
**civil wars and rebellions** · the **1994 genocide against the Tutsi** in Rwanda ·
**tribalism and nepotism** · **corruption** · **poverty, unemployment and illiteracy** ·
**refugees** · heavy **foreign debts** · dependence on the export of raw materials · the
collapse of the old EAC in 1977.

## 4. DEMOCRATIC RULE IN EAST AFRICAN COUNTRIES

### Definitions

**Democracy:** Government of the people, by the people and for the people — a system in which people choose their leaders and take part in how they are governed.

**Citizen:** A full member of a country who owes it loyalty and enjoys its rights and protection.

### The democratic process

Free and fair **elections** held regularly · **freedom of speech, assembly and the press** ·
respect for **human rights** and the **rule of law** · separation of powers between the
**executive, legislature and judiciary** · accountability of leaders · involving people in
decisions at village, district and national level.

### Political and civic education

**Civic education** teaches citizens about their country, their constitution, their leaders,
their rights and their duties, so that they can take part wisely. It is done through
schools, radio, seminars, posters, drama and the Electoral Commission.

### How one becomes a citizen

By **birth** · by **descent** (having a parent who is a citizen) · by **registration** (for
example through marriage or long residence) · by **naturalisation**.

### Rights and responsibilities of citizens

| Basic rights | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| The right to **life** | Obeying the laws of the country |
| The right to **education** | **Paying taxes** honestly |
| The right to **own property** | Protecting public property and the environment |
| **Freedom of speech, worship and association** | **Voting** in elections |
| The right to **health care** | Respecting the rights of others |
| The right to a **fair trial** | Defending the country and keeping peace |
| The right to **vote and to be voted for** | Reporting crime and being a good neighbour |
| Freedom of **movement** | Working hard and caring for one's family |

### The electoral process

1. **Voter registration** and display of the voters' register.
2. **Nomination** of candidates by the Electoral Commission.
3. **Campaigning** within the given period and rules.
4. **Polling day** — voters queue, are checked against the register, are marked with ink, and cast a **secret ballot**.
5. **Counting** the votes at the polling station in the presence of agents and observers.
6. **Declaring the results** by the returning officer and the Electoral Commission.
7. **Swearing in** of the winner; complaints go to the courts.

### Challenges of the electoral process

**Rigging** and stuffing of ballot boxes · **bribery** of voters · **violence and
intimidation** · **illiteracy**, so some voters cannot read the ballot · late delivery of
materials and late opening of polling stations · **low voter turnout** · mistakes in the
voters' register · shortage of **funds** · misuse of state resources · buying of voters'
cards.

### Solutions

Use a **strong, independent Electoral Commission** · use a **clean register** and modern
identification · provide **civic and voter education** · deploy **observers, agents and
police** · punish rigging and bribery · deliver materials on time · count and announce
results openly · settle disputes through the **courts** rather than violence.

# KEY DEFINITIONS

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Explorer | A person who travels to an unknown place to find out about it |
| Protectorate | A country protected and controlled by a stronger country, ruled through its own leaders |
| Colony | A country ruled directly by a foreign power |
| Indirect rule | Ruling a colony through the existing African chiefs |
| Independence | Freedom of a country to govern itself |
| Republic | A country whose head of state is an elected president |
| Democracy | Government of the people, by the people and for the people |
| Citizen | A full member of a country with rights and duties |
| Electoral process | The steps followed in choosing leaders through voting |
| Rigging | Cheating in an election |

# IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- **Speke and Grant** reached the source of the Nile at Jinja in **1862**.
- The **Berlin Conference (1884–85)** shared out East Africa.
- Uganda was a **Protectorate**; Kenya was a **Colony**; Tanganyika was German, then British.
- **Rwanda and Burundi** were ruled by Germany, then **Belgium**.
- Independence dates: **Tanganyika 9 December 1961 · Uganda 9 October 1962 · Rwanda and Burundi 1 July 1962 · Kenya 12 December 1963**.
- Tanganyika and Zanzibar united on **26 April 1964** to form **Tanzania**.
- The **Mau Mau** was the armed struggle in Kenya; **TANU** led Tanganyika; **UPC, DP and KY** were Uganda's parties.
- A **secret ballot** means no one else knows how you voted.
- The **Electoral Commission** organises elections.
