## 1. KEY MEANINGS

### Definitions

**Pan-Africanism:** The feeling and movement of uniting all African people and promoting the political, economic and social interests of Africa.

**Pan-Africanist:** A person who works for the unity and freedom of all people of African origin, wherever they live.

**Nationalism:** A deep love and pride for one's country and the desire to see it free and self-governing.

**Nationalist:** A person who fights for the independence of his or her own country.

**Patriotism:** Love for and loyalty to one's country, shown through good actions.

**Independence:** Freedom of a country to govern itself without being ruled by a foreign power.

### Important Points

- A Pan-Africanist works for **the whole of Africa**; a nationalist works mainly for **his own country**.
- Pan-Africanism began outside Africa, among people of African origin in America and the West Indies who had suffered slavery and racial discrimination.
- The first Pan-African Conference was held in **London in 1900**, organised by **Henry Sylvester Williams**.

---

## 2. LEADING PAN-AFRICANISTS

| Pan-Africanist | Country of origin | Remembered for |
|---|---|---|
| **Henry Sylvester Williams** | Trinidad | Organised the first Pan-African Conference in London, 1900 |
| **W. E. B. Du Bois** | America | Organised several Pan-African Congresses; often called the father of Pan-Africanism |
| **Marcus Garvey** | Jamaica | Preached "Africa for the Africans"; started the "Back to Africa" movement |
| **Booker T. Washington** | America | Fought for the education and dignity of black people |
| **Edward Blyden** | Liberia (born in the West Indies) | Wrote about African personality and pride in being African |
| **J. E. K. Aggrey** | Ghana | Educationist who preached cooperation between black and white people |
| **Leopold Sedar Senghor** | Senegal | Poet and first President of Senegal; developed the idea of *Negritude* |
| **Kwame Nkrumah** | Ghana | Led Ghana to independence in 1957 and worked for the unity of Africa |

### Work of the Pan-Africanists

- Organised **conferences and congresses** to demand freedom for Africa.
- Wrote **books, newspapers and articles** attacking colonial rule and racism.
- Encouraged Africans to be **proud of their colour, culture and continent**.
- **Educated** and inspired African students studying abroad, who later became nationalists.
- Demanded an end to **racial discrimination and colonialism**.
- Their ideas led to the formation of the **OAU** in 1963.

### Methods used by the Pan-Africanists

- Holding **meetings and conferences**.
- Using the **media** — newspapers, books, magazines and speeches.
- Forming **associations and movements**.
- **Lobbying** governments and international bodies.

### Problems faced by the Pan-Africanists

- **Lack of unity** among African leaders.
- **Lack of enough funds** to run their activities.
- **Differences in political ideologies** — some wanted immediate unity, others wanted a slow approach.
- Language barriers between English-speaking and French-speaking Africans.
- **Opposition and arrest** by colonial governments.
- The great **distance** between members who lived in different continents.

## 3. THE COUNTRIES THAT WERE NOT COLONISED

The two African countries that were never colonised are **Ethiopia** and **Liberia**.

### Why Ethiopia kept its independence

- It had a **strong, united government** under Emperor Menelik II.
- It had **modern weapons** bought from Europe.
- Its **mountainous terrain** made invasion difficult.
- Ethiopians were **united** by a common religion and a long history.
- It **defeated Italy at the Battle of Adowa in 1896**.

*(Italy occupied Ethiopia briefly from 1936 to 1941, but Ethiopia was never made a colony.)*

### Why Liberia kept its independence

- It was founded in the 1800s as a **home for freed slaves** from America.
- It was **supported and protected by the United States of America**.
- Other European powers avoided it in order not to quarrel with America.
- It declared itself an **independent republic in 1847**.

## 4. WHY AFRICANS WANTED INDEPENDENCE

- To **control their own national resources** — land, minerals and crops.
- To **stop exploitation** by the colonial rulers.
- To **revive African culture** and pride.
- To **regain land** taken by European settlers.
- To **stop oppression**, forced labour and heavy taxes.
- To gain **social freedom and their rights**.
- To regain **African dignity and self-respect**.
- To ensure **human equality**.
- To **end racial discrimination**.
- To choose their own **leaders and government**.

Other causes that helped:

- **The Second World War** — African soldiers who fought abroad returned with new ideas and confidence.
- The **education** of Africans, who read about freedom and democracy.
- The **independence of India in 1947** and of Ghana in 1957, which inspired others.
- Support from the **United Nations**, which encouraged self-rule.
- The work of the **Pan-Africanists**.

### Methods used by the nationalists

- Forming **political parties** — for example, the Convention People's Party in Ghana and the Uganda People's Congress.
- Forming **trade unions** and organising **strikes**.
- Forming **nationalistic associations** and welfare societies.
- Writing in **newspapers** and holding public rallies.
- **Boycotts** of foreign goods, shops and buses.
- Sending **petitions and delegations** to the colonial governments and to Britain.
- **Armed struggle**, where peaceful means failed — Algeria, Kenya (Mau Mau), Mozambique, Angola, Zimbabwe.
- Taking part in **elections** once they were allowed.

### Problems faced by the nationalists

- **Death** — some were killed in the struggle.
- **Imprisonment and detention** without trial.
- **Harsh laws** made by the colonial governments.
- **Loss of property**, which was confiscated.
- **Intimidation** of them and their families.
- **Oppression** and the banning of their parties and newspapers.
- **Lack of funds** and of modern weapons.
- **Disunity** among the Africans themselves, and tribalism.
- **Illiteracy** among the people they were leading.

## 6. SYSTEMS OF GOVERNMENT AFTER INDEPENDENCE

### Definitions

**Political party:** An organised group of people with similar ideas who want to form the government.

**Multiparty system:** A system of government in which many political parties are allowed to compete for power.

**Single party system:** A system in which only one political party is allowed to exist and to rule.

### Advantages of a multiparty system

- There is room to **choose better leaders**.
- There is **respect for fundamental human rights**.
- There is respect for **hierarchy** and the rule of law.
- There is **delegation of power**.
- Opposition parties **check and control** those in leadership.
- **Government organs are positively developed**.
- People are free to express different opinions.

### Disadvantages of a multiparty system

- It can cause **confusion, quarrels and conflicts**.
- **Favouritism and corruption** may increase.
- Political parties **divide people**, sometimes along tribal lines.
- **Injustice** may increase.
- Decisions and programmes **take long** to be worked on.
- More **election petitions** are raised.
- Elections are **expensive** to conduct.

### Advantages of a single party system

- **Leadership is respected**.
- There is **less conflict** among the people.
- There is **less rigging and few petitions**.
- It is **cheaper to conduct elections**.
- It is **easy to take decisions**.
- The country may stay **united** and avoid tribal politics.

### Disadvantages of a single party system

- It **encourages dictatorship**.
- **Decisions take long to be implemented** because no one questions them.
- There is **less accountability**.
- **New ideas are not accepted**.
- People have **no choice** of leaders.
- Human rights may be **abused** with nobody to complain to.

- **W. E. B. Du Bois** — father of Pan-Africanism. **Marcus Garvey** — "Africa for the Africans".

- **Namibia (1990)** was the last African country to gain independence from colonial rule; **South Sudan (2011)** is the newest state.

- Nationalists' methods: parties, trade unions, strikes, boycotts, petitions, media, armed struggle.
