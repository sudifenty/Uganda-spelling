## 1. ELECTRICITY

### Definition

**Electricity:** A form of energy caused by the movement of tiny charged particles called electrons.

### Sources of electricity

| Source | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Dry cells and batteries** | Chemicals inside produce electricity |
| **Hydro-electric power (HEP)** | Falling water turns turbines — Nalubaale, Kiira, Bujagali, Isimba, Karuma |
| **Solar panels** | Change sunlight into electricity |
| **Wind (windmills)** | Moving air turns a turbine |
| **Generators and dynamos** | Fuel or movement turns a coil inside a magnet |
| **Geothermal** | Heat from inside the earth |
| **Thermal (fossil fuels)** | Burning coal, oil or gas to make steam |
| **Biogas** | Gas from rotting waste is burnt to run a generator |
| **Lightning** | Natural electricity in the sky |

### Types of electricity

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Static electricity** | Electricity that stays in one place | A comb rubbed on dry hair picks up small pieces of paper; lightning |
| **Current electricity** | Electricity that flows through a conductor | Electricity from a cell or from the mains |

Current electricity is of two kinds: **direct current (d.c.)**, which flows one way, as
from a dry cell, and **alternating current (a.c.)**, which keeps changing direction, as
from the mains supply.

## 2. CONDUCTORS AND INSULATORS

### Definitions

**Conductor:** A material that allows electricity to pass through it.

**Insulator:** A material that does not allow electricity to pass through it.

| Conductors | Insulators |
|---|---|
| Copper, aluminium, iron, steel, gold, silver | Rubber, plastic, dry wood, glass, dry cloth |
| Graphite (pencil lead), the human body, salty water | Porcelain, ceramics, dry air, paper |

**Why it matters:** wires are made of **copper** (a good conductor) but are covered with
**rubber or plastic** (an insulator) to keep people safe.

## 3. ELECTRIC CIRCUITS

### Definition

**Electric circuit:** The complete path along which electric current flows.

A simple circuit needs: a **source** (cell), **connecting wires**, a **switch** and an
**appliance** such as a bulb.

| Circuit | How it is arranged | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed (complete)** | No gaps | Current flows and the bulb lights |
| **Open** | A gap or an off switch | No current flows |
| **Series** | Bulbs one after another in one path | If one bulb blows, all go off; bulbs are dimmer |
| **Parallel** | Bulbs on separate branches | If one blows, the others stay on; bulbs are brighter |

### Short circuits

**Short circuit:** When the current finds a shorter path of very little resistance and
flows without passing through the appliance.

**Causes:** worn out or damaged insulation, loose or bare wires touching, water reaching wires, overloading a socket, poor wiring.

**Dangers:** it causes overheating, fires, damaged appliances, electric shock and blown fuses.

### Safety precautions

- Never touch electrical wires, switches or appliances with **wet hands**.
- Never put **metal objects** into a socket.
- Do not overload a socket with many appliances.
- Repair or replace **worn out wires**; do not use naked wires.
- Switch off the mains before repairing anything.
- Keep children away from sockets; use socket covers.
- Do not fly kites near power lines or stand under them during a storm.
- Report fallen power lines; never touch them.
- Use **fuses and circuit breakers**, and earth (ground) appliances.

### Importance of electricity

Lighting homes, schools and streets · cooking and heating water · running machines and industries · pumping and treating water · powering hospitals, fridges and incubators · charging phones and running computers and the internet · running radios and televisions · welding and metal work · creating jobs and small businesses (salons, welding, video halls).

## 4. MAGNETISM

### Definitions

**Magnet:** A material that attracts iron and other magnetic materials.

**Magnetic materials:** Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.

**Non-magnetic materials:** Wood, plastic, copper, aluminium, glass, paper.

### Types of magnets

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural magnets** | Found in the ground | **Lodestone (magnetite)**, the earth itself |
| **Artificial magnets** | Made by people | Bar magnet, horseshoe magnet, ring magnet, needle magnet |
| **Temporary magnets** | Lose their magnetism quickly | Soft iron, electromagnets |
| **Permanent magnets** | Keep their magnetism for a long time | Steel magnets |

### Properties of magnets

- A magnet has two poles: **north** and **south**, and the pull is strongest at the poles.
- **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.** Repulsion is the surest test for a magnet.
- A freely hanging magnet always rests pointing **north–south** — this is how a compass works.
- Magnetism can pass through thin non-magnetic materials such as paper and glass.

### Making temporary magnets

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Stroking** | Stroke a steel bar with one pole of a magnet, always in the same direction |
| **Induction** | Place a magnetic material near a strong magnet |
| **Electrical method (electromagnet)** | Wind insulated wire round a soft iron nail and pass current through it |

### Losing magnetism (demagnetisation)

Heating strongly · hammering or dropping a magnet repeatedly · passing alternating current through it · storing magnets carelessly.

**Storing magnets:** keep bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles together and place soft
iron **keepers** across the ends.

### Generating electricity with a dynamo

A **dynamo** changes movement into electricity. When a coil of wire is turned inside a
magnetic field (or a magnet is turned near a coil), electricity is produced. This is used
on bicycles to light the lamp and, in a much bigger form, in power stations.

### The electric bell

The bell uses an **electromagnet**. When the switch is pressed, current flows and the
electromagnet attracts the soft iron armature, so the hammer strikes the gong. As the
armature moves, it breaks the circuit, the electromagnet loses its magnetism and the
spring pulls the armature back — closing the circuit again. This happens over and over,
so the bell rings continuously.

### Uses of magnets

Compasses for direction · loudspeakers, radios and telephones · electric bells · dynamos and generators · electric motors · cranes for lifting scrap metal · separating iron from other materials · fridge and cupboard door catches · magnetic cards and computer parts · picking up dropped pins.

### Electricity and magnetism in the modern world of work

They run factories and machines, welding and metal work, transport (electric trains and
cars), communication (phones, radio, internet), medical equipment in hospitals, and they
create many jobs — electricians, engineers, technicians, phone repairers and solar
installers.

# IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- Two types of electricity: **static** and **current**.
- Copper is used for wires; rubber and plastic are used to cover them.
- In a **series** circuit, one blown bulb switches off all the others; in a **parallel** circuit, the others stay on.
- **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.** Repulsion is the surest test for magnetism.
- Magnetic materials: **iron, steel, nickel, cobalt**.
- **Lodestone** is a natural magnet.
- Soft iron makes **temporary** magnets; steel makes **permanent** magnets.
- An electric bell works using an **electromagnet**.
- Never touch electrical fittings with **wet hands**.

# EXAMINATION POINTS

- Instrument that changes movement into electricity — dynamo (or generator).
- Natural magnet — lodestone.
- Surest test for a magnet — repulsion.
- Metal used for electric wires — copper.
- Material used to cover wires — rubber or plastic.
- Circuit in which one bulb going off leaves the others on — parallel.
- Main cause of short circuits — worn out or bare wires touching.
- Device that protects a circuit from too much current — a fuse.

# ANSWERS TO REVISION QUESTIONS

1. Electricity is a form of energy caused by the movement of electrons. Its two types are **static electricity** and **current electricity**.
2. Any four of: dry cells, hydro-electric power, solar panels, wind, generators, geothermal, thermal (fossil fuels), biogas.
3. A conductor allows electricity to pass through it, for example copper; an insulator does not, for example rubber.
4. *Learner's drawing.* It should show a cell, connecting wires, a switch and a bulb forming a complete (closed) loop.
5. In a series circuit the bulbs are in one path, so if one blows all go off and the bulbs are dimmer. In a parallel circuit each bulb is on its own branch, so if one blows the others stay on and the bulbs are brighter.
6. A short circuit is when current finds a shorter path of very little resistance and flows without passing through the appliance. Causes: worn out or bare wires touching, water reaching the wires, overloading a socket, poor wiring.
7. Any four of: never touch switches with wet hands; do not put metal objects in sockets; do not overload sockets; replace worn out wires; switch off the mains before repairs; keep children away from sockets; never touch fallen power lines.
8. Any four of: lighting, cooking, ironing, refrigeration, pumping water, running radios and televisions, charging phones, running computers.
9. Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.
10. Natural magnets are found in the ground, for example lodestone; artificial magnets are made by people, for example a bar magnet.
11. **Like poles repel and unlike poles attract.**
12. Place the steel bar on a table. Take one pole of a strong magnet and stroke the bar from one end to the other, always in the **same direction**, lifting the magnet high at the end of each stroke. Repeat many times; the bar becomes a magnet.
13. Any three of: heating it strongly, hammering or dropping it repeatedly, passing alternating current through it, storing it carelessly.
14. Bar magnets are stored in pairs with unlike poles facing each other and soft iron keepers placed across the ends.
15. When the switch is pressed, current flows through the electromagnet, which attracts the soft iron armature so that the hammer hits the gong. This movement breaks the circuit, the electromagnet loses its magnetism and the spring pulls the armature back, closing the circuit again. The action repeats quickly, so the bell rings continuously.
