## A. INTRODUCTION TO DEBATING

**1. What is a debate?**

A debate is a formal discussion in which two sides give different opinions or
arguments about a particular topic or motion.

**2. What is debating?**

Debating is the activity of discussing a topic by giving arguments for or against it.

**3. Why do people debate?**

People debate to express their opinions, give reasons for their views, listen to
other people's views and try to convince others.

**4. What is the main purpose of a debate?**

The main purpose of a debate is to present and defend arguments about a given motion.

**5. What is an argument?**

An argument is a reason or set of reasons given to support or oppose an idea.

**6. What does it mean to argue?**

To argue means to give reasons for or against an idea.

**7. What is an opinion?**

An opinion is what a person thinks or believes about something.

**8. What is a point in a debate?**

A point is an idea or reason given by a speaker to support or oppose a motion.

**9. What is a motion?**

A motion is the statement or topic that is put forward for discussion in a debate.

**10. Give an example of a motion.**

"School uniforms should be compulsory."

**11. What does a motion normally require?**

A motion normally requires people to either support or oppose it.

**12. What does it mean to support a motion?**

To support a motion means to agree with it and give reasons why it should be accepted.

**13. What does it mean to oppose a motion?**

To oppose a motion means to disagree with it and give reasons why it should not be
accepted.

## B. PEOPLE INVOLVED IN A DEBATE

**14. Who is a speaker?**

A speaker is a person who presents arguments during a debate.

**15. Who is a proposer?**

A proposer is a speaker who supports the motion.

**16. Who is an opposer?**

An opposer is a speaker who disagrees with or opposes the motion.

**17. Who is the chairperson?**

The chairperson is the person who controls and guides the debate.

**18. What are the duties of the chairperson?**

The chairperson:

- introduces the motion;
- introduces the speakers;
- gives speakers permission to speak;
- keeps order;
- makes sure speakers follow the rules;
- controls the debate;
- allows appropriate points to be raised;
- ensures that speakers do not exceed their time;
- announces the conclusion of the debate.

**19. Who is the secretary?**

The secretary is the person who keeps records and notes of what happens during the
debate.

**20. What are the duties of the secretary?**

The secretary:

- records important points;
- records the names of speakers;
- takes notes during the debate;
- keeps records of the proceedings;
- records the final result where necessary.

**21. Who is the timekeeper?**

The timekeeper is the person who keeps track of the time given to each speaker.

**22. What are the duties of the timekeeper?**

The timekeeper:

- watches the time;
- tells speakers when their time is nearly finished;
- signals when the speaker's time has ended;
- helps the debate keep to its timetable.

**23. Who is the audience?**

The audience is the group of people listening to the debate.

**24. What is the role of the audience?**

The audience listens carefully, follows the arguments and may participate according
to the rules of the debate.

**25. Why are different roles important in a debate?**

Different roles help the debate to run in an organised and orderly way.

## C. ROLES OF THE SIDES

**26. What are the two main sides in a debate?**

The two main sides are:

1. the proposing side;
2. the opposing side.

**27. What does the proposing side do?**

The proposing side supports the motion and gives arguments in its favour.

**28. What does the opposing side do?**

The opposing side rejects the motion and gives arguments against it.

**29. What should a speaker do before presenting an argument?**

A speaker should understand the motion, research the topic and prepare strong points.

**30. What makes a good argument?**

A good argument should be clear, relevant, logical and supported by good reasons or
evidence.

**31. Should a speaker insult another speaker?**

No. A speaker should attack the argument, not insult the person.

**32. How should speakers respond to opposing views?**

They should respond politely, clearly and respectfully.

## D. RULES OF DEBATING

**33. Why are rules important in a debate?**

Rules help the debate to remain orderly, fair and respectful.

**34. How should a speaker behave during a debate?**

A speaker should:

- speak clearly;
- listen carefully;
- respect other speakers;
- remain calm;
- use polite language;
- stay on the topic;
- give relevant arguments;
- observe the time limit.

**35. Should speakers interrupt one another?**

No. Speakers should not interrupt unless the rules of the debate allow an
appropriate point to be raised.

**36. Should a speaker shout at other speakers?**

No. A speaker should speak clearly and confidently without shouting unnecessarily.

**37. Why should a speaker listen to other speakers?**

Listening helps the speaker understand opposing arguments and respond appropriately.

**38. What should a speaker do when he or she disagrees with another speaker?**

The speaker should politely explain why the argument is not convincing and give a
better reason.

**39. Why should a speaker remain on the topic?**

Remaining on the topic keeps the debate relevant and meaningful.

## E. IMPORTANT DEBATE TERMS

**40. What is a point of order?**

A point of order is a formal interruption made when a speaker believes that the
rules or proper procedure of the debate are not being followed.

**41. Give an example of a point of order.**

"Point of order, Madam Chairperson. The speaker has moved away from the motion."

**42. What is a point of information?**

A point of information is a brief question or piece of information directed to a
speaker according to the rules of the debate.

**43. Give an example of a point of information.**

"Point of information: Can the speaker explain where that information came from?"

**44. What is a point of inquiry?**

A point of inquiry is a request for clarification or further information about
something being discussed.

**45. Give an example of a point of inquiry.**

"Point of inquiry: What does the speaker mean by this statement?"

**46. What is the difference between a point of order and a point of information?**

A point of order concerns the rules or procedure of the debate, while a point of
information seeks or gives information.

**47. What is the difference between a point of information and a point of inquiry?**

A point of information provides or asks for relevant information, while a point of
inquiry mainly seeks clarification or explanation.

## F. PREVIOUS AND CURRENT

**48. What does "previous" mean?**

Previous means something that happened or existed before the present time.

**49. Give a sentence using "previous".**

"The previous speaker gave a strong argument."

**50. What does "current" mean?**

Current means something that is happening or existing at the present time.

**51. Give a sentence using "current".**

"The current speaker is presenting his final point."

**52. What is a previous speaker?**

A previous speaker is someone who spoke before the person who is currently speaking.

**53. What is the current speaker?**

The current speaker is the person who is speaking at the present time.

## G. CONCLUDING A DEBATE

**54. What does "conclude" mean?**

To conclude means to bring something to an end.

**55. What does it mean to conclude a debate?**

To conclude a debate means to bring the discussion to an end after the arguments
have been presented.

**56. Who may conclude a debate?**

The chairperson may formally conclude the debate.

**57. What should a speaker do when concluding an argument?**

The speaker should briefly summarise the main points and show why the motion should
be supported or rejected.

**58. Why is a conclusion important?**

A conclusion reminds the audience of the main arguments and brings the speaker's
presentation to an end.

## H. PREPARING FOR A DEBATE

**59. What should you do before a debate?**

You should:

1. understand the motion;
2. identify whether you are supporting or opposing it;
3. research the topic;
4. collect useful information;
5. prepare your points;
6. arrange your points logically;
7. prepare examples or evidence;
8. practise speaking.

**60. Why should a speaker research a motion?**

Research helps the speaker obtain correct information and strong arguments.

**61. Why should points be arranged logically?**

Logical arrangement makes arguments easier to understand and follow.

**62. Why should a speaker practise before a debate?**

Practice improves confidence, fluency, pronunciation and audibility.

**63. What is audibility?**

Audibility is the ability to speak loudly and clearly enough for listeners to hear.

**64. What is fluency?**

Fluency is the ability to speak smoothly and confidently without unnecessary
hesitation.

**65. What is articulation?**

Articulation is the clear pronunciation of words when speaking.

## I. HOW TO GIVE GOOD ARGUMENTS

**66. What should a good speaker do when giving an argument?**

A good speaker should:

- state the point clearly;
- explain the point;
- give a reason;
- give an example or evidence where appropriate;
- connect the argument to the motion.

**67. Why are examples useful in a debate?**

Examples help the audience understand and believe an argument.

**68. Why is evidence useful?**

Evidence provides support for an argument.

**69. What is a strong argument?**

A strong argument is one that is clear, relevant, logical and well supported.

**70. What is a weak argument?**

A weak argument is one that is unclear, irrelevant, unsupported or illogical.

**71. What should a speaker avoid during a debate?**

A speaker should avoid:

- insults;
- unnecessary repetition;
- irrelevant points;
- false information;
- shouting;
- personal attacks;
- interrupting improperly;
- going beyond the time allowed.

## J. RESPONDING TO ARGUMENTS

**72. What does it mean to respond to an argument?**

It means to answer, challenge or comment on an argument made by another speaker.

**73. How can you politely disagree with a speaker?**

You can say:

- "I respectfully disagree with that point."
- "I do not agree with the argument because…"
- "The previous speaker has overlooked the fact that…"

**74. How can you agree with a speaker?**

You can say:

- "I agree with the previous speaker."
- "That is a good point."
- "I support that argument because…"

**75. How can you challenge an argument?**

You can explain why the argument is weak and provide another reason or evidence.

**76. Should you respect an opposing speaker?**

Yes. Debating requires learners to listen to and respect different opinions.

**77. Why is respect important in debating?**

Respect allows people with different opinions to discuss issues peacefully and
constructively.

## K. OPINIONS

**78. What is an opinion?**

An opinion is a personal belief, view or judgement about something.

**79. How can you introduce your opinion in a debate?**

You can say:

- "In my opinion…"
- "I believe that…"
- "I think that…"
- "In my view…"

**80. Is an opinion always a fact?**

No. An opinion is a person's view, while a fact is something that can be shown to
be true.

**81. Why should an opinion be supported by reasons?**

Reasons make an opinion more convincing.

## L. DIRECT AND INDIRECT SPEECH

The Primary Six curriculum includes the use of **direct and indirect speech** in
debating.

**82. What is direct speech?**

Direct speech gives the exact words spoken by a person.

**83. Give an example of direct speech.**

John said, "I support the motion."

**84. What punctuation is commonly used in direct speech?**

Quotation marks are used to show the exact words spoken.

**85. What is indirect speech?**

Indirect speech reports what someone said without using the person's exact words.

**86. Give an example of indirect speech.**

John said that he supported the motion.

**87. Change this sentence into indirect speech: Mary said, "I support the motion."**

Answer: Mary said that she supported the motion.

**88. Change this sentence into indirect speech: Peter said, "The motion is important."**

Answer: Peter said that the motion was important.

**89. Change this sentence into direct speech: Sarah said that she supported the motion.**

Answer: Sarah said, "I support the motion."

**90. Why is direct speech useful in debating?**

It can show the exact words spoken by a person.

**91. Why is indirect speech useful in debating?**

It can be used to report what another speaker said.

## M. LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: EVEN THOUGH

**92. What does "even though" show?**

"Even though" shows contrast between two ideas.

**93. Give an example using "even though".**

"Even though the previous speaker gave many points, I disagree with the motion."

**94. Complete the sentence: "Even though she was nervous, ______."**

Answer: "Even though she was nervous, she presented her argument confidently."

**95. Make a sentence using "even though" and "debate".**

"Even though the debate was difficult, the speaker remained confident."

## N. LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: ALTHOUGH

**96. What does "although" show?**

"Although" also shows contrast between two ideas.

**97. Give an example using "although".**

"She presented strong arguments although some people disagreed with her."

**98. Complete the sentence: "Although he was young, ______."**

Answer: "Although he was young, he gave a strong argument."

**99. Make a sentence using "although" about a debate.**

"Although the opposing side disagreed, the proposer continued to defend the motion."

## O. LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: IF

**100. What does "if" show?**

"If" introduces a condition.

**101. Give a sentence using "if" in a debate.**

"If I were the chairperson, I would give every speaker enough time."

**102. Complete the sentence: "If I were the speaker, ______."**

Answer: "If I were the speaker, I would explain my argument clearly."

**103. Complete the sentence: "If I were the chairperson, ______."**

Answer: "If I were the chairperson, I would keep the debate orderly."

## P. LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: WHEREAS

**104. What does "whereas" show?**

"Whereas" is used to show a contrast between two people, things or ideas.

**105. Give an example using "whereas".**

"The proposer supports the motion, whereas the opposer rejects it."

**106. Complete the sentence: "John supports the motion, whereas ______."**

Answer: "John supports the motion, whereas Peter opposes it."

**107. Make a sentence using "if" and "whereas".**

"If I were the chairperson, I would allow both sides to speak, whereas some
chairpersons may be too strict."

## R. HOW A DEBATE CAN BE CONDUCTED

**118. What is the first step in conducting a debate?**

A motion is selected and clearly stated.

**119. What happens after the motion is stated?**

The chairperson introduces the debate and the speakers.

**120. What does the proposer do?**

The proposer presents arguments supporting the motion.

**121. What does the opposer do?**

The opposer presents arguments against the motion.

**122. What happens as the debate continues?**

Speakers from both sides present their arguments and respond to opposing views.

**123. What does the chairperson do throughout the debate?**

The chairperson controls the debate, maintains order and ensures that the rules are
followed.

**124. What does the secretary do during the debate?**

The secretary makes important notes and keeps records.

**125. What does the timekeeper do?**

The timekeeper monitors the time used by each speaker.

**126. What does the audience do?**

The audience listens to the arguments and follows the debate.

**127. How does a debate end?**

The speakers finish presenting their arguments and the chairperson concludes the
debate according to the rules.

## S. MAKING NOTES DURING A DEBATE

**128. Why should learners make notes during a debate?**

Notes help learners remember the important arguments and information presented.

**129. What should you write when making debate notes?**

You should write:

- the motion;
- the names or sides of speakers;
- important arguments;
- supporting evidence;
- opposing arguments;
- important responses;
- the conclusion.

**130. Should you write every word spoken?**

No. You should record the important points rather than every word.

**131. What is a good way of making debate notes?**

Use short points, key words and brief phrases.

## T. INTERPRETING A MOTION

**132. What does it mean to interpret a motion?**

It means to understand clearly what the motion is saying.

**133. Why should a speaker understand a motion?**

Understanding the motion helps the speaker give relevant arguments.

**134. What should you ask yourself when interpreting a motion?**

Ask:

- What is the motion about?
- What does it mean?
- Am I supporting or opposing it?
- What reasons support my side?
- What arguments might the other side give?

**135. Give an example of a motion.**

"Children should help with work at home."

**136. What would the proposer do with this motion?**

The proposer would give reasons why children should help with work at home.

**137. What would the opposer do?**

The opposer would give reasons why children should not be required to help with
work at home, or would challenge the way the motion is stated.

## U. FORMULATING A MOTION

**138. What does it mean to formulate a motion?**

It means to create or prepare a statement for discussion in a debate.

**139. What makes a good motion?**

A good motion should:

- be clear;
- be understandable;
- be suitable for debate;
- allow people to support or oppose it;
- concern an issue that can be discussed.

**140. Give examples of suitable debate motions for Primary Six.**

1. "School uniforms should be compulsory."
2. "Children should help with housework."
3. "Homework should be given every day."
4. "All schools should have libraries."
5. "Pupils should not carry mobile phones to school."

**141. Why should a motion be clear?**

A clear motion prevents confusion about what is being debated.

## V. LIFE SKILLS AND VALUES IN DEBATING

**142. What life skills are developed through debating?**

Debating develops:

- effective communication;
- fluency;
- audibility;
- articulation;
- confidence;
- responding to questions;
- self-esteem;
- assertiveness.

**143. How does debating improve communication?**

It teaches learners to express ideas clearly and listen to others.

**144. How does debating build confidence?**

It gives learners opportunities to speak before other people.

**145. What is self-esteem?**

Self-esteem is the way a person values and feels about himself or herself.

**146. How does debating develop self-esteem?**

It gives learners opportunities to express their ideas and opinions.

**147. What is assertiveness?**

Assertiveness is the ability to express your views and rights confidently and
respectfully.

**148. How does debating develop assertiveness?**

Learners practise expressing their points of view and responding to questions.

**149. What values are developed through debating?**

Debating develops values such as:

- respect;
- appreciation;
- tolerance;
- confidence;
- fairness;
- listening to others.

**150. Why should we respect people with different opinions?**

People have different experiences and views. Respect allows them to discuss their
differences peacefully.

## IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- A **debate** is a formal discussion; **debating** is the activity of arguing for
  or against a motion.
- People in a debate: **speaker, proposer, opposer, chairperson, secretary,
  timekeeper, audience** — know each role and duty.
- The three formal points: **point of order** (rules), **point of information**
  (information), **point of inquiry** (clarification).
- **Previous** = before now; **current** = happening now; **conclude** = bring to
  an end.
- Preparation: understand the motion, research, collect information, prepare and
  arrange points logically, prepare examples, practise speaking (**audibility,
  fluency, articulation**).
- A good argument is **clear, relevant, logical and well supported**; attack the
  **argument**, never the person.
- **Even though / although** show contrast; **if** introduces a condition;
  **whereas** contrasts two people or things.
- **Direct speech** quotes exact words in quotation marks; **indirect speech**
  reports without them ("said that he supported the motion").
- Debate notes use **short points, key words and brief phrases** — never every word.
- Debating builds **communication, confidence, self-esteem, assertiveness** and
  values of **respect, tolerance and fairness**.

## EXAMINATION POINTS

- Learn the KEY DEFINITIONS table word for word — **debate, motion, proposer,
  opposer, chairperson, secretary, timekeeper, point of order, point of
  information, point of inquiry** are the favourites.
- "Who…?" role questions: the **chairperson** controls, the **secretary** keeps
  records, the **timekeeper** watches time, the **audience** listens.
- Direct/indirect speech conversions are guaranteed — quotation marks disappear and
  tenses shift back ("is important" → "was important").
- Structure items must use the exact connector: **even though / although**

- Be ready with the **duties lists** (chairperson nine, secretary five, timekeeper
  four) and what makes a **strong vs weak argument**.
- Interpret-before-argue: always ask what the motion means and which side you are on.
