# TOPIC 2: DEBATE

*Curriculum reference: NCDC Primary Six Curriculum, Set One, English Topic 2, 36 periods

**What you already know:** You have already met the debate. In P.6 you now master it
fully — the people and their duties, the debate vocabulary, how to prepare and
argue, the language structures of debate talk, and the skills of a good debater.

**By the end of this topic you should be able to:**

- explain a debate, a motion and the roles of everyone who takes part
- use the debate vocabulary: argument, point, opinion, point of order,
  point of information, point of inquiry
- prepare for a debate, interpret and formulate motions, and rebut arguments
- use the structures **even though, although, if, whereas** and direct and
  indirect speech in debate talk
- make notes during a debate and give a good salutation

## MEANING OF A DEBATE

**1. What is a debate?**

A debate is a formal discussion in which people present arguments for or against a
particular motion or topic.

**2. What is a motion?**

A motion is a statement or proposal that is presented for discussion in a debate.

**3. Give an example of a motion.**

"Pupils should be allowed to use mobile phones at school."

## PEOPLE IN A DEBATE AND THEIR DUTIES

**4. What are the two main sides in a debate?**

The two main sides are:

- The **proposing side**
- The **opposing side**

**5. Who is a proposer?**

A proposer is a person who supports and argues in favour of the motion.

**6. Who is an opposer?**

An opposer is a person who disagrees with the motion and argues against it.

**7. What is a speaker?**

A speaker is a person who presents arguments or ideas during a debate.

**8. Who is the chairperson?**

The chairperson is the person who controls and guides the debate.

**9. What are the duties of a chairperson?**

The chairperson:

- Introduces the motion.
- Introduces the speakers.
- Gives speakers permission to speak.
- Maintains order.
- Ensures that the rules of the debate are followed.
- Controls the proceedings of the debate.
- Gives speakers their turns.
- Announces the end of the debate.

**10. Who is a secretary in a debate?**

A secretary is the person responsible for keeping records and taking important
notes during the debate.

**11. What are the duties of a secretary?**

The secretary:

- Records important points.
- Takes notes during the debate.
- Records the names of speakers where necessary.
- Keeps the records of the debate.

**12. Who is a timekeeper?**

A timekeeper is a person who keeps track of the time given to each speaker.

**13. What is the role of a timekeeper?**

The timekeeper:

- Keeps track of speaking time.
- Warns speakers when their time is nearly over.
- Signals when the speaker's time has ended.

**14. Who is the audience?**

The audience consists of the people who listen to the debate.

**15. What is the role of the audience?**

The audience listens to the arguments, follows the debate and may take part in
voting or responding when allowed.

## DEBATE VOCABULARY

**16. What is an argument?**

An argument is a reason or set of reasons given to support or oppose an idea.

**17. What is a point?**

A point is an idea or reason given by a speaker to support an argument.

**18. What does it mean to argue?**

To argue means to give reasons to support or oppose an idea.

**19. What is an opinion?**

An opinion is a person's belief, view or judgement about something.

**20. What does "conclude" mean?**

To conclude means to bring something to an end or give a final statement.

**21. What does "previous" mean?**

Previous means something that happened or was mentioned earlier.

**22. What does "current" mean?**

Current means something that is happening or existing now.

**23. What is a point of order?**

A point of order is a formal interruption made by a speaker to draw attention to a
rule or procedure that is not being followed.

**24. What is a point of information?**

A point of information is a brief question or piece of information directed to a
speaker during a debate according to the rules of the debate.

**25. What is a point of inquiry?**

A point of inquiry is a question raised to seek clarification or further
information about something being discussed.

## PEOPLE AND THEIR ROLES IN A DEBATE

**26. Mention the people who may take part in a debate.**

They may include:

- Chairperson
- Proposers
- Opposers
- Secretary
- Timekeeper
- Audience

**27. What is the role of the proposer?**

The proposer supports the motion and gives arguments explaining why the motion
should be accepted.

**28. What is the role of the opposer?**

The opposer disagrees with the motion and gives arguments explaining why it should
not be accepted.

**29. Why should speakers research before a debate?**

Speakers should research so that they can collect accurate information and give
strong arguments.

**30. Why should speakers prepare points before debating?**

Preparation helps speakers organise their ideas and present clear and convincing
arguments.

## HOW TO PREPARE FOR A DEBATE

**31. How should a learner prepare for a debate?**

A learner should:

1. Understand the motion.
2. Know which side they are supporting.
3. Research the topic.
4. Identify strong points.
5. Find examples to support the points.
6. Think about possible arguments from the other side.
7. Prepare responses to opposing arguments.
8. Organise the points logically.
9. Practise speaking clearly.
10. Keep within the given time.

**32. Why should a debater understand the motion?**

Understanding the motion helps the debater give relevant arguments instead of
discussing unrelated ideas.

**33. What should a debater do before choosing points?**

The debater should understand the meaning of the motion and research the topic.

**34. Why should arguments be supported with examples?**

Examples make arguments clearer and more convincing.

## RULES OF DEBATING

**35. Mention some rules that should be followed during a debate.**

- Listen carefully to other speakers.
- Do not insult other speakers.
- Do not interrupt unnecessarily.
- Respect different opinions.
- Speak clearly.
- Remain relevant to the motion.
- Follow the chairperson's instructions.
- Keep within the time given.
- Use polite language.
- Give reasons for your opinions.

**36. Why should a debater listen to other speakers?**

Listening helps the debater understand opposing arguments and respond appropriately.

**37. Why should debaters respect one another?**

Respect promotes peaceful discussion and allows everyone to express their views
freely.

**38. Why should a speaker keep within the time given?**

A speaker should keep within the time so that all participants get an opportunity
to speak.

## ARGUING FOR AND AGAINST A MOTION

**39. What does it mean to argue for a motion?**

It means giving reasons why the motion should be accepted.

**40. What does it mean to argue against a motion?**

It means giving reasons why the motion should not be accepted.

**41. What is a supporting argument?**

A supporting argument is a reason that agrees with and strengthens a particular
position.

**42. What is an opposing argument?**

An opposing argument is a reason that disagrees with another person's position.

**43. What is a counterargument?**

A counterargument is an argument given in response to an opposing argument.

**44. What is rebuttal?**

A rebuttal is a response that challenges or disproves an argument presented by the
other side.

**45. Why is rebuttal important in a debate?**

Rebuttal allows a speaker to respond to opposing ideas and defend their position.

## EXAMPLE OF A MOTION

**46. Consider the motion:**

"Pupils should be given homework every day."

**47. Give points for the motion.**

Points for the motion may include:

1. Homework helps pupils practise what they learn.
2. Homework helps pupils revise their lessons.
3. Homework can improve pupils' academic performance.
4. Homework encourages pupils to study independently.
5. Homework helps teachers identify areas where pupils need help.

**48. Give points against the motion.**

Points against the motion may include:

1. Too much homework may make pupils tired.
2. Pupils need time to rest and play.
3. Some pupils may not have help at home.
4. Too much homework may reduce time for family activities.
5. Pupils may become stressed by excessive homework.

## INTERPRETING A MOTION

**49. What does it mean to interpret a motion?**

To interpret a motion means to understand exactly what the statement being debated
means.

**50. Why is it important to interpret a motion correctly?**

It helps speakers give arguments that are directly related to the motion.

**51. What should a learner ask when interpreting a motion?**

The learner should ask:

- What is the motion about?
- What does each important word mean?
- Am I supporting or opposing it?
- What reasons can support my position?
- What arguments might the other side give?

## FORMULATING A MOTION

**52. What does it mean to formulate a motion?**

To formulate a motion means to create or write a statement that can be debated.

**53. What makes a good motion?**

A good motion should:

- Be clear.
- Be understandable.
- Have two possible sides.
- Be suitable for discussion.
- Be relevant to the learners or community.
- Allow people to give arguments for and against it.

**54. Give examples of suitable debate motions.**

- Pupils should wear school uniforms.
- School children should be given homework.
- Children should help with housework.
- Television is more useful than radio.
- Every child should learn how to use a computer.

## RESPONDING TO ARGUMENTS

**55. How should a learner respond to an argument?**

A learner should listen carefully, understand the argument and respond politely
with a relevant reason or counterargument.

**56. What should a learner avoid when responding to an argument?**

A learner should avoid:

- Insults.
- Personal attacks.
- Irrelevant points.
- Shouting.
- Interrupting unnecessarily.
- Giving unsupported claims.

**57. Give an example of a polite response to an opposing argument.**

"Although the previous speaker has made a good point, I disagree because..."

## MAKING NOTES DURING A DEBATE

**58. Why should a learner make notes during a debate?**

Notes help the learner remember important arguments and respond to points made by
other speakers.

**59. What should a learner write down during a debate?**

A learner may write:

- Main points.
- Important examples.
- Arguments from the opposing side.
- Points that need to be challenged.
- Useful facts.
- Important conclusions.

**60. What should good debate notes be like?**

Good debate notes should be:

- Short.
- Clear.
- Relevant.
- Well organised.
- Easy to understand.

## SALUTATIONS IN A DEBATE

**61. What is a salutation?**

A salutation is a polite form of greeting or addressing people.

**62. How can a speaker begin a formal debate?**

A speaker may say:

"Mr/Madam Chairperson, members of the panel, fellow debaters and members of the
audience..."

**63. Why are salutations important in a debate?**

They show respect and help the speaker begin the debate in a formal and polite way.

## DIRECT AND INDIRECT SPEECH

**64. What is direct speech?**

Direct speech gives the exact words spoken by a person.

**65. Give an example of direct speech.**

The speaker said, "I support the motion."

**66. What punctuation is commonly used in direct speech?**

Quotation marks are used to show the exact words spoken.

**67. What is indirect speech?**

Indirect speech reports what someone said without using their exact words.

**68. Give an example of indirect speech.**

The speaker said that he supported the motion.

**69. Change this sentence into indirect speech: The teacher said, "The debate will begin at ten o'clock."**

Answer: The teacher said that the debate would begin at ten o'clock.

**70. Change this sentence into direct speech: The speaker said that she supported the motion.**

Answer: The speaker said, "I support the motion."

## LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: EVEN THOUGH

**71. How is "even though" used?**

**Even though** is used to show a contrast between two ideas.

**72. Give an example using "even though".**

Even though the previous speaker made a good point, I disagree with the argument.

**73. Complete the sentence: "Even though the speaker was nervous, ______."**

Answer: he presented his points clearly.

## LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: ALTHOUGH

**74. How is "although" used?**

**Although** is used to show a contrast between two ideas.

**75. Give an example using "although".**

She argued out her points although the audience did not agree with her.

**76. Complete the sentence: "Although the speaker disagreed with me, ______."**

Answer: I respected her opinion.

## LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: IF

**77. What does "if" express?**

**If** is used to introduce a condition.

**78. Give an example using "if" in a debate.**

If I were the chairperson, I would allow the speaker to continue.

**79. Complete the sentence: "If I were the chairperson, ______."**

Answer: I would maintain order in the debate.

## LANGUAGE STRUCTURE: WHEREAS

**80. What does "whereas" mean?**

**Whereas** is used to show a contrast between two people, things or ideas.

**81. Give an example using "whereas".**

The proposer supports the motion, whereas the opposer disagrees with it.

**82. Complete the sentence: "The proposer supports the motion, whereas ______."**

Answer: the opposer disagrees with it.

## COMBINING IF AND WHEREAS

**83. Give a sentence using "if" and "whereas".**

If I were the chairperson, I would allow both sides to speak, whereas some
chairpersons may give more time to one side.

## GOOD DEBATING SKILLS

**84. What makes a good debater?**

A good debater:

- Understands the motion.
- Researches the topic.
- Gives clear arguments.
- Uses relevant examples.
- Listens carefully.
- Responds appropriately.
- Speaks confidently.
- Respects other speakers.
- Uses polite language.
- Keeps within the time.

**85. Why is confidence important in debating?**

Confidence helps a speaker present arguments clearly and effectively.

**86. Why is research important in debating?**

Research gives a speaker information and evidence that can be used to support
arguments.

**87. Why is clear speech important?**

Clear speech enables the audience and other debaters to understand the speaker's
ideas.

**88. Why should a debater remain relevant to the motion?**

Remaining relevant ensures that the arguments directly address the topic being
debated.

## BENEFITS OF DEBATING

**89. How does debating help learners?**

Debating helps learners to:

- Develop confidence.
- Improve speaking skills.
- Improve listening skills.
- Develop critical thinking.
- Improve reasoning skills.
- Develop research skills.
- Improve vocabulary.
- Learn to express opinions.
- Learn to respect different opinions.
- Develop leadership skills.

**90. How does debating improve communication?**

It gives learners opportunities to speak, listen, explain ideas and respond to
others.

**91. How does debating develop critical thinking?**

Learners have to examine different ideas, identify strong and weak arguments and
make reasoned responses.

**92. How does debating develop leadership?**

Learners practise speaking confidently, organising ideas, making decisions and
taking responsibility.

## SAMPLE DEBATE

**93. What is the motion for this sample debate?**

"Children should help with housework."

**94. Give points for the motion.**

1. Housework teaches children responsibility.
2. It helps children develop useful skills.
3. It teaches children to work with others.
4. It reduces the workload of parents.
5. It prepares children for independent living.

**95. Give points against the motion.**

1. Too much housework may interfere with schoolwork.
2. Children need time to rest and play.
3. Some household tasks may be dangerous for children.
4. Young children may not have the skills to perform certain tasks.
5. Parents should ensure that children are not overworked.

**96. Give an example of a conclusion for the debate.**

In conclusion, children can benefit from helping with suitable household tasks,
provided that the work is safe, reasonable and does not interfere with their
education and health.

## IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- A debate is a **formal discussion** with two sides: the **proposing side** and the **opposing side**.
- The **chairperson** controls the debate; the **secretary** keeps records; the **timekeeper** watches the time; the **audience** listens and may vote.
- A **point of order** concerns the **rules**; a **point of information** is a brief question **allowed by the rules**; a **point of inquiry** seeks **clarification**.
- A **counterargument** answers an opposing argument; a **rebuttal** challenges or disproves it.
- **Interpret** a motion before arguing: understand what it means and which side you are on.
- A good motion is **clear, understandable, has two possible sides** and allows arguments for and against.
- Begin formally with a **salutation**: "Mr/Madam Chairperson, members of the panel, fellow debaters and members of the audience…"
- **Even though / although** show contrast; **if** introduces a condition; **whereas** contrasts two people or things.
- **Direct speech** quotes exact words in quotation marks; **indirect speech** reports without them ("said that he supported the motion").
- Good debate notes are **short, clear, relevant, well organised and easy to understand**.

## EXAMINATION POINTS

- Learn the KEY DEFINITIONS word for word — **debate, motion, proposer, opposer, chairperson, secretary, timekeeper, point of order, point of information, point of inquiry, rebuttal** are the favourites.
- "Duties/roles" questions: give **five** duties of the chairperson, **four** responsibilities of the secretary, **three** tasks of the timekeeper.
- Direct/indirect speech conversions are guaranteed — remember: quotation marks disappear and tenses shift back ("will begin" → "would begin").
- Structure items must use the exact connector: **even though / although** (contrast), **if** (condition), **whereas** (contrast between two sides).
- Be ready with **five rules of debating**, **five qualities of a good debater** and the **ten steps** of preparation.
- For/against motion questions: pick one side and keep every point on **that** side.
