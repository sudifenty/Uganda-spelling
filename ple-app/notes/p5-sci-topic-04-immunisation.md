# TOPIC 4: IMMUNISATION

*Curriculum reference: NCDC Primary Five Curriculum, Set One, Integrated Science, 20 periods
(`curriculum/P5-SET-ONE-2010.pdf`, topic outline page 38 and pages 44–47).*

**By the end of this topic you should be able to:**

- explain what immunity is and name its types
- state the importance of immunity
- name the immunisable childhood diseases and their vaccines
- describe the immunisation schedule and the care of vaccines
- state the importance of immunisation and the problems that hinder it

---

## 1. IMMUNITY

**Immunity:** The ability of the body to fight and resist disease.

| Type | How it is got | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural immunity** | The body's own defence, without medicine | The skin, tears, saliva, stomach acid, **white blood cells**; the **antibodies a baby gets from the mother** through the placenta and through **breast milk (colostrum)**; the immunity left after recovering from a disease such as measles |
| **Acquired (artificial) immunity** | Given by people, through **vaccination** | Polio drops, BCG, measles vaccine and the others below |

**Importance of immunity:** it protects the body from disease · it reduces death,
especially of children · it saves money spent on treatment · it keeps children in school and
adults at work · it stops epidemics from spreading.

---

## 2. IMMUNISATION AND VACCINES

**Immunisation:** Giving a person a vaccine so that the body can make antibodies and resist a disease.

**Vaccine:** A medicine, usually made from weakened or killed germs, given to protect the body against a disease.

### The immunisable childhood diseases named in the syllabus

| Disease | Vaccine | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tuberculosis (TB)** | **BCG** | Given at birth, on the right upper arm; leaves a small scar |
| **Polio** | **Polio drops (OPV)** and injection (IPV) | Given at birth and with each DPT dose; polio causes paralysis |
| **Diphtheria, Whooping cough, Tetanus** | **DPT** (now given as a **pentavalent** vaccine) | Three doses at 6, 10 and 14 weeks |
| **Hepatitis B** | Included in the **pentavalent** vaccine | Protects the liver |
| **Haemophilus influenzae type b** | Included in the **pentavalent** vaccine | Prevents meningitis and pneumonia |
| **Measles** | **Measles vaccine** | Given at 9 months, with a second dose later |

Other vaccines given in Uganda today include those against **pneumonia (PCV)**,
**rotavirus** (severe diarrhoea), **HPV** (cancer of the cervix, for girls) and
**tetanus for expectant mothers**.

### The child immunisation schedule (as taught in primary school)

| Age | Vaccine |
|---|---|
| **At birth** | BCG and polio (0) |
| **6 weeks** | Polio 1, pentavalent 1, PCV 1 |
| **10 weeks** | Polio 2, pentavalent 2, PCV 2 |
| **14 weeks** | Polio 3, pentavalent 3, PCV 3 |
| **9 months** | Measles (with vitamin A) |

Every child is given a **child health card (immunisation card)**, which the parent must keep
safely and bring to every visit.

---

## 3. CARE OF VACCINES — THE COLD CHAIN

Vaccines are **spoilt by heat and by strong light**, so they must be kept cold from the
factory to the child. This is called the **cold chain**.

They are kept in a **refrigerator or cold box with ice packs**, carried in a **vaccine
carrier**, checked with a **thermometer**, kept out of sunlight, and **never used after the
expiry date** or once the vial monitor shows they are spoilt.

---

## 4. IMPORTANCE OF IMMUNISATION

- It **prevents killer diseases** and their disabilities, such as the paralysis of polio.
- It **reduces infant and child deaths**.
- It is **cheaper than treatment**.
- It keeps children **healthy, growing and in school**.
- It has **wiped out smallpox** and almost wiped out **polio** in Uganda.
- It protects the whole community when most children are immunised.

## 5. PROBLEMS THAT HINDER IMMUNISATION, AND SOLUTIONS

| Problem | Solution |
|---|---|
| Ignorance and **rumours** about vaccines | Health education on radio, in schools and by village health teams |
| **Distance** to health centres and poor roads | Outreach clinics and mobile teams |
| **Power failure** spoiling vaccines | Solar fridges, cold boxes and ice packs |
| Some parents **forget** or lose the card | Reminders by village health teams; keep the card safely |
| **Insecurity** and hard-to-reach areas | Special campaigns and community volunteers |
| Shortage of **funds, staff and vaccines** | Government and partner support, better planning |
| **Religious or cultural objections** | Dialogue with leaders and correct information |

# KEY DEFINITIONS

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Immunity | The ability of the body to resist disease |
| Natural immunity | Immunity the body has on its own or gets from the mother |
| Acquired immunity | Immunity got through vaccination |
| Immunisation | Giving a vaccine so the body can resist a disease |
| Vaccine | A medicine made from weakened or killed germs |
| Antibody | A substance made by the body to fight germs |
| Cold chain | Keeping vaccines cold from the factory to the child |
| BCG | The vaccine against tuberculosis |
| Pentavalent | One vaccine protecting against five diseases |
| Immunisation card | The card showing the vaccines a child has received |

---

# IMPORTANT FACTS TO REMEMBER

- Two types of immunity: **natural** and **acquired**.
- **BCG** protects against **tuberculosis** and is given **at birth**, leaving a scar on the right upper arm.
- **Polio** is given as drops, starting at birth.
- **DPT/pentavalent** is given at **6, 10 and 14 weeks**.
- **Measles** is given at **9 months**.
- Pentavalent protects against **diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, hepatitis B and Hib**.
- Vaccines must be kept cold — the **cold chain**.
- Immunisation wiped out **smallpox**.

# EXAMINATION POINTS

- The ability of the body to resist disease → **immunity**.
- Immunity a baby gets from breast milk → **natural** immunity.
- The vaccine given at birth against TB → **BCG**.
- Where BCG is given → the **right upper arm**.
- The disease that causes paralysis in children → **polio**.
- Age at which measles vaccine is given → **9 months**.
- Keeping vaccines cold from factory to child → the **cold chain**.
- The card showing the vaccines a child has received → the **immunisation (child health) card**.

6. Which five diseases are covered by the pentavalent vaccine?
7. At what ages are polio and pentavalent vaccines given?
8. At what age is the measles vaccine given?

10. Give any four ways of caring for vaccines.
11. What is an immunisation card, and why must it be kept safely?
12. Give any five importances of immunisation.

# ANSWERS TO REVISION QUESTIONS

1. Immunity is the ability of the body to fight and resist disease. Its two types are **natural** and **acquired (artificial)** immunity.
2. Any three of: the skin, tears, saliva and stomach acid; white blood cells; antibodies a baby gets from the mother and from breast milk; immunity left after recovering from a disease.
3. **Immunisation** is giving a person a vaccine so that the body can make antibodies and resist a disease. A **vaccine** is a medicine, usually made from weakened or killed germs, given to protect the body.
4. Any six of: tuberculosis, polio, diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, measles, hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b (also pneumonia and rotavirus today).
5. **BCG**, given **at birth**, on the right upper arm.
6. **Diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, hepatitis B and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib).**
7. Polio at **birth, 6, 10 and 14 weeks**; pentavalent at **6, 10 and 14 weeks**.
8. At **9 months** (with a later second dose).
9. The cold chain is the system of **keeping vaccines cold from the factory to the child**. It is necessary because vaccines are **spoilt by heat and strong light** and would then fail to protect the child.
10. Any four of: keep them in a refrigerator or cold box with ice packs; carry them in a vaccine carrier; check the temperature with a thermometer; keep them out of sunlight; never use them after the expiry date or when the vial monitor shows they are spoilt.
11. It is the card that shows **all the vaccines a child has received and the dates**. It must be kept safely so that health workers know which doses are still due, and it is often needed when joining school.
12. Any five of: it prevents killer diseases and disabilities; it reduces child deaths; it is cheaper than treatment; it keeps children healthy and in school; it has wiped out smallpox; it protects the whole community.
13. **Smallpox.**
14. Any five of: ignorance and rumours; long distances and poor roads; power failure spoiling vaccines; parents forgetting or losing the card; insecurity in some areas; shortage of funds, staff or vaccines; religious or cultural objections.
15. **Rumours** — health education by radio, schools and village health teams; **long distance** — outreach and mobile clinics; **power failure** — solar fridges, cold boxes and ice packs; **forgetting** — reminders from village health teams and keeping the card where it is easily seen.
